# How to Write a Research Paper in 40 Steps

*A complete, practical workflow — from working title to polished submission.*
*Adapted and expanded from Hengl, T. and Gould, M. (2002). Rules of thumb for writing research articles.*

---

## How to Use This Guide

A strong research paper is not written from start to finish in one pass. It is built in layers.

Follow the steps in order: first you define what you are doing and why it matters, then you describe how you did it, then you report what you found, then you interpret what it means. You write the abstract near the end, even though it appears first in the finished paper. You then revise for logic and polish for precision.

The structure follows the classic **IMRaD** format — **Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion** — with title, abstract, revision, and polishing stages around it.

Keep this principle throughout: **Every sentence should either advance your argument, provide necessary evidence, or help the reader follow your reasoning. If it does none of these, cut it.**

---

## Part 1: Title

### Step 1: Start With a Working Title That Guides Your Research

Write a clear, working title before you write anything else. It does not need to be final, but it should act as a compass.

A good working title names your topic, your object of study, and — when possible — your approach or key variable. It should be specific enough to keep you focused and searchable enough that another researcher could find it.

**Example pattern:** *[Method / Intervention] + [Effect on / Study of] + [Population / System / Context]*

> Example: *Evaluating Machine Learning Models for Early Detection of Crop Disease in Smallholder Farms in East Africa*

Revisit and refine the title at the end. Your final title should reflect what you actually found, not just what you planned to find.

## Part 2: Introduction

Your introduction should take the reader from broad context to a precise, justified research question in six to eight paragraphs. Think of it as a funnel: wide at the top, narrow at the bottom.

### Step 2: Introduce Your Topic Clearly and Define Key Terminology

Open with a concise, engaging statement of your topic. In the first paragraph, the reader should know exactly what field you are working in and what problem you are addressing.

Define any technical terms, abbreviations, or concepts that are central to your paper the first time you use them. Do not assume the reader shares your exact definition. A clear definition early prevents confusion later.

### Step 3: Explain Why the Topic Is Significant

Answer the reader's first question: *Why should I care?*

Explain the scientific, practical, social, economic, or theoretical importance of your topic. Use concrete evidence — a gap in knowledge, a real-world problem, a cost, a risk, or an unresolved debate — rather than vague claims like "this is very important."

If your research could influence policy, practice, theory, or future research, say how.

### Step 4: Connect Your Work to Existing Knowledge

Summarize the most relevant prior research, not everything ever published on the topic.

Group the literature by theme, method, or finding, rather than listing studies one by one. Show what is already established, where scholars agree, and where findings diverge. This demonstrates that you understand the conversation you are joining.

Cite primary sources where possible, and be fair to work that disagrees with your view.

### Step 5: Identify the Gap

State clearly what is missing, unresolved, or underexplored.

A gap can be:

- A population, context, or condition that has not been studied
- A contradiction between previous findings
- A method that has not been applied to this problem
- A theoretical explanation that has not been tested
- A practical problem that existing research does not solve

Make the gap explicit. Do not leave the reader to infer it. One or two precise sentences are more powerful than a long, vague critique.

### Step 6: Formulate Clear Research Questions

Translate the gap into one to three focused research questions.

Good research questions are specific, answerable, and directly connected to the gap you identified. They should define what you will investigate, not what you hope to prove.

Avoid questions that are too broad ("How does climate change affect agriculture?") or too narrow to matter. A strong question implies its method:

> Example: *To what extent does early-season satellite monitoring improve the accuracy of yield prediction compared to ground-based surveys?*

### Step 7: State Your Purpose, Objectives, and Hypotheses

Close the introduction by stating what you aim to achieve and how you will achieve it.

- **Purpose:** The overall aim of the study in one sentence.
- **Objectives:** The specific, measurable steps you will take to achieve that aim.
- **Hypotheses / Expectations:** If applicable, what you expect to find and why, based on theory or prior evidence.

End with a brief roadmap sentence: what the reader will find in the rest of the paper.

## Part 3: Methods

Your methods section must allow a competent researcher in your field to understand — and in principle, reproduce — what you did. Clarity and completeness matter more than elegance here.

### Step 8: Outline Your Methodological Steps

Begin with a concise overview of your research design. State whether your study is experimental, observational, computational, qualitative, mixed-methods, theoretical, or a review, and why that design is appropriate for your research questions.

Then list the main stages of your work in logical order: data collection, processing, analysis, and validation. This overview acts as a map for the detailed description that follows.

### Step 9: Explain the Theory Behind Your Chosen Methodology

Do not just name a method — justify it.

Explain the theoretical or statistical principles that underpin your approach, and why it is suitable for your object of study. If you chose one method over alternatives, briefly explain the trade-off.

Cite foundational sources for your methods. This connects your work to established practice and shows that your choices were deliberate, not arbitrary.

### Step 10: Describe Your Experimental Setup in Detail

Provide a complete, precise description of your setup:

- Study site, population, sample, or dataset, including size and selection criteria
- Instruments, equipment, software, and versions used
- Materials, conditions, and parameters
- Procedures, protocols, and timeline
- Ethical approvals, consent, or permissions, if applicable

Include enough detail that the reader can judge the quality of your work and replicate the procedure. Use subheadings, tables, and diagrams where they improve clarity.

### Step 11: Define Your Object of Study Precisely

Describe exactly what you examined — and what you did not.

Specify your study organism, system, material, dataset, case, or phenomenon, including its key characteristics, boundaries, and variables. Define your independent, dependent, and control variables, and how each was measured or manipulated.

If you excluded certain data, cases, or conditions, state the criteria and the reason.

---

## Part 4: Results

Results report what you found. They do not yet interpret what it means. Keep interpretation for the Discussion.

### Step 12: Summarize Your Results

Begin with a high-level summary of your main findings, in words, before presenting detailed tables and figures.

State the overall pattern first, then support it with evidence. Lead with the result, not the method: *"Yield prediction accuracy increased by 18% when satellite data were included"* is stronger than *"We ran a model and obtained..."*

Present results in the same order as your objectives and research questions so the reader can follow the logic.

### Step 13: Compare Different Outcomes

Show how your outcomes relate to each other.

Compare groups, conditions, time periods, models, or scenarios side by side. Use tables and figures to make comparisons visible at a glance, and use text to highlight the comparisons that matter most.

Do not repeat every number from a table in the text. Highlight the pattern, the magnitude of the difference, and whether it is statistically or practically meaningful.

### Step 14: Focus on Your Main Discoveries

Distinguish between primary findings and secondary observations.

Give the most space to the results that directly answer your research questions. Secondary or exploratory findings can be reported briefly, but they should not distract from the central story.

If a result is surprising or counterintuitive, report it accurately. Do not hide it or soften it to fit your expectations.

### Step 15: Answer Your Research Questions With Your Findings

Return explicitly to each research question from Step 6 and answer it.

For each question, state: what you found, how strong the evidence is, and any uncertainty or nuance. If a question cannot be fully answered, say why and what would be needed to answer it.

This step creates the bridge to your Discussion — the reader should now know exactly what you discovered before you explain what it means.

---

## Part 5: Conclusion and Discussion

This is where you interpret, contextualize, and evaluate your work. A good Discussion moves from your specific findings outward to broader implications.

### Step 16: Support and Defend Your Conclusions With Evidence

State your conclusions directly, and tie each one to specific evidence from your Results.

Avoid overstating. A conclusion should be no stronger than the data allow. Use cautious, precise language when the evidence is suggestive rather than conclusive: *"suggests," "is consistent with," "provides evidence for"* rather than *"proves."*

If you make a claim, the reader should be able to trace it back to a result, a table, or a figure.

### Step 17: Explain Conflicting or Unexpected Findings

Do not ignore results that contradict your hypothesis or prior literature.

Discuss possible explanations: methodological limitations, sample characteristics, measurement error, contextual differences, or a genuine flaw in existing theory. Consider alternative interpretations fairly.

Addressing contradictions honestly strengthens your paper. It shows intellectual rigor and helps future researchers avoid the same pitfalls.

### Step 18: Reflect on the Strength and Significance of Your Findings

Evaluate what your findings add.

Are they robust across conditions? Are they practically meaningful as well as statistically significant? Do they confirm, extend, challenge, or refine existing knowledge?

Distinguish between a finding that is statistically significant and one that matters in the real world. Explain the size of the effect and its relevance, not just its p-value.

### Step 19: Acknowledge the Limitations of Your Study

State the limitations clearly and specifically.

Common limitations include small sample size, limited geographic scope, measurement constraints, assumptions in a model, potential biases, or lack of long-term data. For each limitation, explain how it might affect your conclusions and what you did to minimize it.

A candid limitations section does not weaken your paper — it makes your conclusions more trustworthy.

### Step 20: Highlight the Importance and Implications of Your Findings

Explain what your work means beyond your specific study.

Discuss implications for theory, methodology, practice, policy, or future research. Who should care about these findings, and what should they do differently as a result?

Be specific. Instead of "this has important implications," write: *"These results suggest that extension services should prioritize early-season monitoring in regions with..."*

### Step 21: Establish How Your Work Introduces New Ideas or Perspectives

Close the Discussion by articulating your contribution.

What is new here? Is it a new method, a new dataset, a new theoretical insight, a new application, or a new synthesis of existing ideas? Position your contribution clearly in relation to the gap you identified in Step 5.

End with a forward-looking sentence: the most important next question your work opens up.

---

## Part 6: Abstract

Write the abstract last, even though it appears first. It should be a complete, standalone summary of the entire paper.

### Step 22: Craft a Concise, Structured Abstract

In 150 to 250 words (or as required by your target journal), cover five elements in order:

1.  **Background:** What is the problem and why does it matter?
2.  **Aim:** What did you investigate?
3.  **Methods:** How did you do it?
4.  **Results:** What did you find? Include key numbers or main patterns.
5.  **Conclusion:** What does it mean?

Use clear, declarative sentences. Avoid citations, abbreviations that are not defined, and vague phrases. A reader should be able to decide from the abstract alone whether to read the full paper.

> **Tip:** Write the abstract after the Discussion is finished, then check that every claim in the abstract appears in the main text.

---

## Part 7: Revision — Checking Logic and Coherence

Revision is not proofreading. It is checking whether your argument holds together.

### Step 23: Check Title vs. Content Alignment

Read your title again after finishing the draft. Does it accurately reflect your actual findings, methods, and scope?

If your focus shifted during the research — which is normal — update the title. The title is a promise to the reader; the paper must keep it.

### Step 24: Ensure Key Terms Are Clear and Consistent

Check that every key term is defined once, early, and then used with the same meaning throughout.

Do not switch between synonyms for the same concept unless you explain the distinction. Inconsistent terminology confuses readers and reviewers.

Create a mental glossary: list your 5–10 core terms and search for each one to verify consistency.

### Step 25: Ensure Objectives and Variables Are Clear and Relevant

Re-read your objectives from Step 7. Are they still clear, specific, and directly addressed in your Methods and Results?

Confirm that every variable you introduce is defined, justified, and actually used. Remove variables, measures, or analyses that do not serve your objectives.

### Step 26: Link All Variables, Techniques, and Measurements to Existing Knowledge

For each method, variable, and measurement, ask: *Have I explained why this is appropriate and how it connects to prior work?*

If a technique is standard in your field, a brief citation is enough. If it is novel or adapted, explain it in more detail and justify the adaptation.

### Step 27: Confirm Reproducibility

Ask yourself: *Could a competent researcher in my field reproduce this study from my description alone?*

Check for missing details: data sources, parameters, software versions, processing steps, sample sizes, exclusion criteria, and statistical tests. If data or code can be shared, state where and how.

Reproducibility is a core standard of science, not an optional extra.

### Step 28: Check for Repetition and Remove Redundancies

Read for duplication across sections.

The Introduction should motivate, the Methods should describe, the Results should report, and the Discussion should interpret. The same finding should not be reported in full in both the Results and the Discussion — summarize and refer back instead.

Cut sentences that repeat the same idea in different words. Tight writing is clearer writing.

### Step 29: Align Every Statement With Your Objectives

Check that every paragraph supports your research questions and objectives.

If a paragraph is interesting but tangential, either cut it, move it to supplementary material, or explicitly connect it to your argument. A focused paper is more persuasive than a sprawling one.

### Step 30: Validate Your Findings

Re-examine your evidence for each claim.

Are your statistical tests appropriate? Are your figures accurate? Do your tables match your text? Have you checked for errors in calculation, transcription, or coding? If you ran sensitivity checks, robustness tests, or validation on independent data, report them.

If a claim rests on a single, fragile result, qualify it or strengthen the evidence.

### Step 31: Ensure Text Coherence

Read the paper as a whole, not section by section.

Check transitions between paragraphs and sections. Each paragraph should have a clear topic sentence, logical development, and a link to the next idea. Use signposting sparingly but effectively: *"In contrast," "Building on this," "Taken together."*

Read the paper aloud if possible. Awkward flow is easier to hear than to see.

### Step 32: Make the Abstract Informative and Standalone

Re-read your abstract on its own, without the paper.

Can a reader understand the problem, method, main result, and conclusion without reading the rest? Does it contain any claim that is not in the main text? Does it use any undefined abbreviation or jargon?

Revise until the abstract can stand alone.

## Part 8: Polishing — Precision and Presentation

Polishing is the final pass for language, formatting, and scholarly accuracy.

### Step 33: Use Appropriate Tenses

Follow the standard conventions of your field:

- **Past tense** for what you did and what you found: *"We collected samples..." "Accuracy increased..."*
- **Present tense** for established knowledge, definitions, and your interpretation: *"Satellite monitoring provides..." "These results suggest..."*
- **Future tense** sparingly, for proposed work: *"Future studies should..."*

Be consistent within each section.

### Step 34: Verify All Equations Are Correct and Relevant

Check every equation for accuracy, notation, and completeness.

Define every symbol the first time it appears. Ensure units are consistent, derivations are correct, and numbering matches in-text references. Remove equations that are not used or do not add value.

If you adapted an equation from another source, cite it and explain any modifications.

### Step 35: Explain Any Abbreviations Used

Define each abbreviation at first use, and use it consistently thereafter.

Avoid over-abbreviating. If a term appears only once or twice, write it out in full. Include a list of abbreviations if your journal requires it or if you use many technical terms.

### Step 36: Prune Word Usage and Avoid Filler

Cut filler words and phrases that add length without meaning: *"very," "really," "in order to," "it is important to note that," "due to the fact that."*

Prefer active, concrete language: *"We measured"* over *"Measurements were taken."* Prefer one precise word over three vague ones.

Aim for clarity, not complexity. Good academic writing is simple and precise, not ornate.

### Step 37: Ensure All Units and Techniques Are Standardized

Use SI units or the standard units of your field, consistently throughout.

Check that measurements, decimal places, significant figures, and formatting are consistent across text, tables, and figures. State any conversions clearly.

If you use a non-standard technique or unit, justify it and provide the standard equivalent.

### Step 38: Assess the Quality and Relevance of Figures and Tables

Review every figure and table. Ask:

- Does it present information more clearly than text alone?
- Is it necessary, or does it duplicate the text?
- Is it legible at publication size, with readable labels and axes?
- Does it use color accessibly and consistently?

Remove decorative or redundant visuals. Keep only figures and tables that advance your argument.

### Step 39: Include All Figures, Tables, and Legends in the Text

Every figure and table must be referenced in the text, numbered in order of appearance, and accompanied by a self-explanatory caption or legend.

A reader should be able to understand a figure from its caption and legend alone, without hunting through the text. State what the figure shows, define symbols and error bars, and note sample sizes.

Check that in-text references (e.g., "Figure 2") match the actual numbering.

### Step 40: Ensure References Are Current, Complete, and Correct

Verify every reference:

- **Current:** Have you cited recent, relevant work, not only older foundational sources?
- **Complete:** Does every in-text citation appear in the reference list, and vice versa?
- **Correct:** Are author names, years, titles, journal names, volumes, pages, and DOIs accurate?
- **Consistent:** Does the formatting follow your target journal's style (APA, IEEE, Nature, etc.)?

Use a reference manager to avoid manual errors, but always proofread the final list. Inaccurate references undermine credibility.

---

## Final Checklist Before Submission

Before you submit, run through this quick check:

- [ ] Title reflects the actual study and findings
- [ ] Abstract is standalone and matches the paper
- [ ] Research questions are stated and answered
- [ ] Methods are detailed enough to reproduce
- [ ] Results are reported without interpretation
- [ ] Discussion interprets, contextualizes, and acknowledges limitations
- [ ] All figures and tables are referenced, captioned, and accurate
- [ ] Terminology, units, and abbreviations are consistent
- [ ] References are complete and correctly formatted
- [ ] Paper has been read aloud once for flow and clarity
